------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A2. From raw fixations to 4 reading-time measures
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
"There are two raw datasets. Dataset 1P gives me FFD directly — one row per
fixation, so for a word with a single fixation, FFD is just that duration.
Dataset 2P records every individual fixation with a fixation number, which
lets me compute three more measures: FPD, first-pass duration, is the sum
of fixations before the eye ever regresses backward. RPD, regression-path
duration, is the sum from the first fixation until the eye moves past the
word to the right. TD, total duration, is every fixation on that word,
including re-reads. Then, because I have 10 subjects, for every word
position I sum all 10 subjects' values — a subject who skipped it
contributes zero — and divide by 10. That average is what actually goes
into the model, not any one person's individual reading time."
[Point at: the per-subject FFD table for the first 3 words of Text 1, then
the resulting dundee_rt.csv sample with ffd/fpd/rpd/td columns]
[~140 words, ~60 sec]


A3. The 6 lexical features, one at a time
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY (feature 1 — wlen):
"Feature 1, word length. This one's the simplest — it's just the character
count of the word, taken directly from the Dundee corpus's own WLEN column.
'Tourists' is 8 characters, so wlen equals 8. It sounds trivial, but it
turns out to be one of the most predictive features in the whole model,
which is actually part of what motivates the Tamil proposal later —
character count is a good proxy for complexity in English, but I'll argue
it may not transfer to other languages."
[Point at wlen column in lexical_features.csv for the printed sample words]

SAY (feature 2 — sent_len):
"Feature 2, sentence length — the number of words in the sentence this word
belongs to. It comes from the Dundee corpus's own sentence files, not
something I compute myself, I just count words per line."
[Point at the printed first/second sentence and their word counts]

SAY (feature 3 — wiki_freq):
"Feature 3, Wikipedia frequency. I use a Python library called wordfreq,
which gives the probability of a word appearing in Wikipedia text. I
convert that to frequency-per-million and take a log, so common words like
'the' get a high score and rare words like 'enticed' get a low one — this
is a standard log-frequency transform used throughout psycholinguistics,
not something I invented."
[Point at the printed P values and resulting wiki_freq for 'the', 'tourists',
'enticed' — note the contrast between a common and a rare word]

SAY (features 4 & 5 — aoa_mean, aoa_std):
"Features 4 and 5, Age of Acquisition — the average age at which a word is
typically learned, from published norms by Kuperman et al. 2012, a 30,000+
word dataset. If a word isn't in that dictionary, I fall back to the corpus
mean rather than leaving it blank, so no word has a missing value."
[Point at aoa.csv row count and the aoa_mean/aoa_std columns in
lexical_features.csv]

SAY (features 6 & 7 — fwd_prob, bwd_prob):
"Features 6 and 7, forward and backward transition probability. These use
the British National Corpus, 100 million words, to ask: given the previous
word, how likely is this word to come next — that's forward. And given the
NEXT word, how likely was this word to have preceded it — that's backward.
I use add-one smoothing so a word pair that never appeared in the BNC still
gets a small non-zero probability instead of breaking the log."
[Point at fwd_prob/bwd_prob columns for the first 5 words of Text 1]
[Combined A3 total: ~280 words, ~2 min]


A4. The 11 syntactic / structural features
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY (features 8 & 9 — lex_surprisal, syn_surprisal):
"Feature 8, lexical surprisal, is closely related to the BNC transition
probability, but expressed as negative log probability — 'how surprised are
you to see this word given the previous one.' Feature 9, syntactic
surprisal, is the more involved one: it comes from a left-corner parser run
over 39,832 parse trees from the Penn Treebank Wall Street Journal
sections. I take each Dundee word's part-of-speech tag from a separate
annotation file, then compute how surprising that POS tag is given the
previous POS tag using bigram statistics from the treebank. For example,
seeing a plural noun right after a present-tense verb is somewhat unusual,
so that gets a higher surprisal score than a more expected tag sequence."
[Point at the annotation file sample — word/pos/head/deprel columns — then
the lex_surprisal/syn_surprisal columns in syntactic_features.csv]

SAY (feature 10 — entropy_red):
"Feature 10, entropy reduction. This measures how much a word narrows down
what could come next. After the word 'United', there are really only a
couple of likely continuations — 'States' or 'Nations' — so uncertainty
drops sharply, and that's a high entropy reduction score. It's computed as
the drop in entropy, the drop in average uncertainty over next-word
probabilities, and clipped at zero so a word that INCREASES uncertainty
doesn't get a negative score."
[Point at entropy_red column for the sample words]

SAY (features 11 & 12 — emb_depth, emb_diff):
"Features 11 and 12 come from the dependency parse tree that's part of the
Dundee corpus's own annotation. Embedding depth is how many hops a word is
from the root of its sentence's tree — the root itself is depth zero, its
direct dependents are depth one, and so on. Embedding difference is just
the change in depth from the previous word to this one — did we go deeper
into the structure or come back up."
[Point at the printed word/POS/head/deprel/depth table — walk through 2-3
words showing how depth is counted from the head chain]

SAY (features 13-17 — h1 through h8):
"The last group, features 13 through 17, are yes/no flags derived from
those depth transitions between adjacent words — did the head change from
the previous word, did depth increase, did it decrease, is this word the
root, did depth jump by more than one level in either direction, or stay
exactly the same. These capture the SHAPE of the syntactic structure around
each word, not just its raw depth number."
[Point at h1/h2/h3/h4/h6/h7/h8 columns for the sample words]
[Combined A4 total: ~290 words, ~2 min]


A5. From features to the System 1 number you already know
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
"Putting it together: all 17 features plus the 4 averaged reading-time
measures get merged into one table, about 50,647 word rows. I split by

SLIDE 4: Method
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
"The method: for every word, compute GPT-2 surprisal — how unpredictable
that word is given everything that came before it, including up to 3
preceding sentences of context so the model isn't guessing cold at the
start of every sentence. Then swap that in for syn_surprisal in the same
19-feature model, same 60/20/20 sentence split, same Ridge regression —
nothing else in the pipeline changes. I tested this across all four
reading-time measures, not just FFD, since the paper's own feature also
gets evaluated against all four."
CODE TO SHOW — full walkthrough:
  experiments/compute_gpt2_surprisal.py, function word_token_surprisals()
  (lines 66-118):
    - lines 74-75: the sentence gets split into words, and prior-sentence
      context gets prepended before it's fed to the model
    - lines 84-91: this is the actual GPT-2 forward pass — for each
      subword token, take negative log probability of that token given
      every token before it. That's the literal definition of surprisal.
    - lines 93-116: subword surprisals get summed back up into one

      by start position you mis-assign the sentence-initial word after a

SLIDE 11: The New Idea
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
"This isn't meant to be a straight port of the English pipeline to a new
language — there's a genuine new research question underneath it. From my
own results, word length is already an unstable complexity proxy even in
English — that's the same Table 1 versus Table 2 inconsistency in the
original paper that I flagged earlier in this project. Tamil is
agglutinative: a single word can carry what English spreads across an
entire clause, with case, tense, and agreement all stacked onto one word
through suffixes. So character-count word length is likely an even WORSE
proxy for complexity there — morphemes, the smallest meaningful units
inside a word, may be the more linguistically correct thing to count
instead of raw characters. Concretely, the plan is: use an open-source
Tamil morphological analyzer, ThamizhiMorph, to decompose each word into
its morphemes and build a morpheme-count-based complexity metric; then use
that metric to drive and evaluate an LLM-based Tamil sentence simplifier,
with the simplified output validated against human readability judgments,
the same basic evaluation logic as System 2, just with morphology
replacing word length as the core complexity signal."

==============================================================================
APPENDIX — ANTICIPATED Q&A (12 questions, prepared answers)
==============================================================================
Use this if he interrupts with a question mid-talk, or offer 2-3 of these
proactively near the end if there's leftover time ("a few things I expect
you might ask — want me to cover any of these?").

Q1: "Why Ridge regression and not something more powerful, like a neural
    network or gradient boosting?"
A: "To stay comparable to the original paper, which uses Ridge — swapping
    the model family would confound whether a difference in results comes
    from the new feature or from a stronger model. It's also more
    interpretable and appropriate for 17-19 features on ~50K rows, where a
    deep model would likely overfit without adding real signal."

Q2: "Could the GPT-2 improvement just be because GPT-2 was trained on data
    that includes Wikipedia or news text similar to Dundee?"
A: "Possible in principle, but the Dundee corpus is British newspaper text
    from the 1990s, and GPT-2's surprisal is being computed word-by-word
    from context within the SAME sentence/document, not retrieving memorized
    text — it's measuring local predictability, not recognition. I haven't
    directly tested for memorization contamination, but the sentence-level
    train/dev/test split at least prevents the SPLIT itself from leaking."

Q3: "Why did you only test up to 3 sentences of prior context for GPT-2,
    not the whole document?"
A: "It's a tradeoff between more context (better predictions) and compute
    time per word — 3 sentences was a practical choice, not something I
    swept exhaustively. That's an easy follow-up experiment if you'd like
    to see the sensitivity curve."

Q4: "Have you tried combining syntactic surprisal AND GPT-2 surprisal,
    instead of swapping one for the other?"
A: "That's the 'gpt2_added' variant in the three-way comparison

Q5: "How do you know the threshold-tuning step (zeroing low predictions)
    isn't just overfitting to the dev set?"
A: "The threshold is selected ONLY on the dev set, then applied unchanged
    to the held-out test set — the reported R² is always on test data the
    threshold was never tuned against."

Q6: "For the Tamil proposal, how would you even get ground-truth
    readability labels without an eye-tracking corpus?"
A: "Two options I'm considering: human readability ratings collected
    directly (Likert-scale judgments on sentence pairs, similar to how the
    Wikipedia/Simple-Wikipedia System 2 labels work), or a Tamil
    parallel-text resource if one exists for standard vs. simplified
    writing. This is one of the open design questions I'd want your input
    on before committing to a data-collection plan."

Q7: "Isn't 'morphemes instead of characters' just swapping one proxy for
    another — how do you know morpheme count is actually better?"
A: "Fair — it's a hypothesis, not a proven result yet. The plan is to test
    it the same way I tested word length here: check whether a
    morpheme-count feature correlates with human readability judgments
    more consistently than character-count word length does on Tamil data,
    the same kind of consistency check I ran for word length in English."

Q8: "What's the biggest technical risk in the Tamil plan?"
A: "Honestly, ThamizhiMorph's coverage and accuracy on real-world text —
    if the morphological analyzer itself is unreliable on informal or
    domain-varied Tamil, the complexity metric built on top of it inherits
    that noise. I'd want to validate the analyzer's output on a sample
    before building the full pipeline on it."

Q9: "Why not test this idea on a language with MORE existing resources
    first, to reduce risk?"
A: "That's a reasonable alternative — Turkish or Finnish have more NLP
    tooling. I chose Tamil specifically because of the genuine literature
    gap (Slide 10) and because it's a language I can validate simplified
    output in myself, which matters for the human-judgment evaluation
    step."

Q11: "Since System 2 is 'effectively closed' in your words, are you sure

A: "There's likely more headroom, but I think it's diminishing returns
    relative to time cost — the paper's remaining FFD advantage may come

Q12: "What would make you abandon the Tamil direction if it doesn't pan

A: "If ThamizhiMorph's output turns out too unreliable to build a metric
    on, or if I can't collect enough human readability judgments to
    validate against, I'd treat that as a signal to scope down to a
    smaller, purely descriptive study (e.g., just characterizing how word
    length vs. morpheme count behave differently on Tamil text) rather than
    pushing through to a full simplifier system."
